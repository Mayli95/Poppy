# Research — Poppy

## 1. Reference forensics

**Liked**

- **100coconuts.com** — sans headlines at 32–48px, all-caps for shout lines only ("KEEP IT REAL, KEEP IT 100!"), body 14–16px. Sparse layout, ~35–40% content coverage per viewport, 40–60px section gaps. Product shot: flat-lay, front-facing, overhead diffused light, no hard shadow, can fills 60–70% of its card. Sticky header, horizontal mega menu (SHOP/LEARN/VARIETY PACK), cart fixed top-right. White ground throughout; color lives almost entirely in the product itself, not the UI. No parallax — confidence comes from scale, not motion.
- **bonbuz.com** — geometric sans, italics for emphasis inside otherwise blunt declarations ("enter the _chrysalis_"). Big gutters, 80–120px between modules. Photography centred, soft diffused light, generous negative space around product (60–70% of frame is void). Vibrant accent color (fuchsia/yellow) appears only as small flourish shapes against white/neutral — restraint is what makes the hit land.
- **mahati.co** — flat, icon-like can renders on solid saturated color fields (pink/blue/green) — no lifestyle staging, catalog-like precision. Color is *functional*: each flavor owns its own field color, which is also how products are told apart at a glance. Nav is 5–6 items max, sticky. This is the closest reference to "color coding as information," which matters for a states-based structure.
- **drinkolipop.com** — cans at 3/4 angle in square frames, bright even overhead light, metallic/label detail legible. Sticky header that compresses on scroll. Shop dropdown is a real taxonomy (by format, by pack). Single accent (burgundy) carries buttons/badges; backgrounds stay neutral. Moderate-high density — this is the competent-mainstream end of "bold."
- **drinkculturepop.com** — lowercase, light-weight headline voice ("not your typical soda"). Single-can hero shots, tight crop, can is the only saturated thing in frame. Carousel for flavors, accordion for subscription detail. 3–4 column grid, generous whitespace.
- **delacalle.mx** — hand-drawn wordmark (deliberately imperfect, not a stock face). Each flavor gets its *own* solid pastel field color behind the product shot (orange for mandarin, pink for prickly pear) — color is a sorting key, same move as Mahati. Tight, angled product crops showing can dimension. Rich product cards (image + name + description + price) without feeling cluttered because the surrounding whitespace absorbs it.
- **Shopify "Taste" theme** — the baseline to beat: bold headline + thin-stroke "industrial" type, mega menu, swatch filters, product-page image zoom/rollover, a nutrition-info block. This is "specialty beverage, competently templated." It never organizes by anything other than product type.

**Disliked**

- **actiphwater.com** — clinical because color is reserved *only* for discount badges; product is shot straight-on, evenly lit, no shadow play, isolated with nothing around it. Long, slow sections. Nothing wrong mechanically — it's a vacuum of decision-making, not a specific sin.
- **drinkmoment.com / sunwink.com / aurabora.com / drinkdaydream.com** — share one thing structurally across all four: saturation is capped low (pastel-only palette), letterspacing is wide and weight is light (softens everything it touches), whitespace is maximal and used as the entire personality, and copy hedges ("gently, steadily," "mild sweetness," "no funny business"). None of these are *badly built* — Daydream's cloud-layering on scroll and Sunwink's accordion FAQ are both clean mechanics. The actual problem is chromatic and verbal: low saturation + hedge-y voice, not sloppy construction. **Overlap finding:** every disliked site uses the *same* generous-whitespace, light-weight-sans, centred-product-shot mechanics as the liked sites (bonbuz and culturepop are just as spacious). The difference the client is actually reacting to is saturation and commitment of voice, not density or layout. This means Poppy should feel free to use calm, spacious layout mechanics — the thing to avoid is turning the color down or hedging the copy.

## 2. Customer language

Real phrasing pulled from juice/kombucha/wellness-shot buyer discussion and shelf-life guidance:

- Shelf life is the recurring anxiety: cold-pressed juice is reported to last "3 to 5 days in the refrigerator," is "at its absolute best within the first 72 hours," and "lasts just 2 hours at room temperature" once out of the cold chain — confirms the brief's instinct that customers get the delivery window wrong and need it stated before checkout, not after.
- Delivery-temperature complaints show up as "melted deliveries," juice that "arrived frozen" or had "fermented by the first day," and charges going through on orders that never showed up — same category of problem Poppy is trying to pre-empt with a hard cutoff and fixed delivery days.
- Kombucha first-timers talk in texture/intensity terms, not flavor terms: "not a trace of vinegar to be found," "light and fruity, more akin to seltzer," "neither too sweet nor too acidic," "an acquired taste." Buyers are trying to gauge *how much of a kombucha experience* they're signing up for, which is exactly the kind of worry a "what does this do for me" framing can defuse before they even read a flavor name.
- Wellness shots get discussed skeptically: "probably won't make any game-changing impact," paired with practical questions about whether ginger/turmeric shots "burn" going down and whether they're worth doing daily. The honest, slightly deflating framing ("tiny amounts do not a healthy diet make") is closer to how an actual regular customer talks than any health-brand copy — Poppy's boosters copy should sound confident about *what the shot does for twenty minutes*, not inflate it into a health claim.

## 3. Category landscape

The default pattern, shared by nearly every direct competitor (Olipop, CulturePop, Pressed, Suja, Raw Generation, JuiceUs): a homepage hero, then a product grid organized **by format** — juice vs. kombucha vs. shots — with "cleanse" or "reset" bundles sold as a separate, secondary collection bolted on top of the format grid, not as the entry point.

Structurally different: **Pressed Juicery** and **Suja** both also run a parallel "shop by benefit" collection (energy, calm, gut health, skin) *alongside* their format grid — proving the benefit-first collection is a recognized pattern in this exact category, but in both cases it's a secondary filter, never the front door. **Monogram Coffee** (adjacent category) is the one example found where benefit/mood selection — "Flavour Mood" — fully replaces the technical taxonomy (origin, roast) as the primary way in. That's the pattern worth stealing, not the juice category's own halfway version of it.

## 4. Out-of-category structure

Five structures from outside beverages that could carry this catalog:

1. **Paint retailers' "shop by color family"** (e.g. C2 Paint) — the entire catalog re-sorted by a perceptual property (hue) rather than by product format (interior/exterior/primer). Direct structural parallel: sort by *what you want*, not by *what it is*.
2. **Supplement "shop by goal"** (GNC, Vitamin Shoppe, Higher Nature) — Energy, Focus, Sleep, Gut Health as the primary taxonomy, with the actual pill/powder format demoted to a secondary attribute on the product page. This is the closest existing analogue to Wake Up / Refuel / Cool Down / Reset.
3. **Perfume "shop by scent family"** (DecanTx) — citrus, fruity, white floral as entry categories, where a single product can legitimately belong to more than one family. Useful precedent for Poppy's stated problem: a kombucha and a smoothie living in the same state.
4. **Monogram Coffee's "Flavour Mood"** — mood-first subscription picker that fully replaces origin/roast as the way in, with format details pushed to a secondary swap/preview step. Closest adjacent-category proof that benefit-first navigation can be the *entire* front door, not a filter.
5. **Ski-resort trail maps (sorted by difficulty/colour rating, not by lift or region)** — a physical, color-coded, at-a-glance system where a single visual key (green/blue/black) tells you everything about fit *before* you commit, with the literal mountain geography as secondary. Direct parallel for using color-as-state the way Poppy's cup sleeves already (almost) do.

## Five findings that change what gets built

1. The four states (Wake Up / Refuel / Cool Down / Reset) have a real precedent *structurally* — supplement "shop by goal" sites and Monogram's Flavour Mood both prove benefit-first navigation can fully replace format taxonomy, not just supplement it. Poppy should go further than Pressed/Suja (who keep it as a secondary filter) and make states the only front door — format never gets its own top-level nav item.
2. Every disliked reference site is disliked for color saturation and hedging voice, not for using whitespace or centred product photography — those mechanics are shared with the liked sites. Poppy can be spacious and still be "loud": the lever to pull is saturation and declarative copy, not density.
3. Mahati and De La Calle both already prove the move Poppy needs: a solid, saturated field color behind/around a product shot used as a sorting signal. This maps directly onto the four states — each state gets one color field that reappears everywhere a product in that state is shown, solving the brief's worry that the four-color sleeve palette and the four states "may be too close to keep separate" by making them intentionally the same system.
4. Shelf life and delivery-day anxiety are real and specific (concrete day-counts, temperature complaints, charged-but-never-arrived stories) — this has to be stated as a fact on the product page itself (pressed-on date, delivery day, cutoff time), not parked in a shipping policy page nobody reads before paying.
5. Kombucha buyers describe products by intensity/texture ("light and fruity," "acquired taste") before they describe them by flavor name. Product copy for anything fermented needs an intensity cue up front, not just a flavor list — this is a copy requirement, not just a structure one.
